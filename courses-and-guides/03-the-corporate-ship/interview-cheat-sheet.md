# The Corporate Ship — Interview Cheat Sheet
### What to say when they ask, and what they actually mean when they ask

**Who this is for:** Any brother crossing from the block into a tech paycheck — first job, career switch, or second chance. **When to read it:** The night before, the morning of, and the hour before. **How to use it:** Every question here is decoded, answered with a pattern, and paired with the follow-up you should expect.

---

## The game, in plain terms

An interview is not an exam. It's a test of whether the manager can picture you at the desk next week. Every question — even the weird ones — is the manager asking one of five things: *Can you do the work? Will you do the work? Can I stand working with you? Will you stay? Will you make me look smart for hiring you?* Your whole job in that room is to hand them the picture, with receipts.

The block's advantage: everything corporate America claims to want — grit, resilience, ownership, drive — you've lived through in conditions these managers studied in school. The cheat sheet's job is translation: turning what you've lived into what they can hear. Not erasing it. Translating it.

## The five questions you WILL be asked

### 1. "Tell me about yourself."

**What they mean:** *Pitch me. Sixty seconds. Why are you in my building?*

**The pattern (the 60-second arc):** Where you started → what you built → why you're here. Three beats, one breath each.

**The answer:**
> "I'm from [city/neighborhood]. I got into tech because [the real reason — 'I wanted to build the stuff I was always using' / 'somebody showed me HTML and I got hooked' / 'I needed options, and code was the one that didn't ask permission']. Since then I've built [receipt: the GitHub Pages site, the sandbox project, the fantasy league stat table — name ONE, with the live link]. I'm here because [the role's actual work] is the direction I'm already building in, and I bring the receipts."

**Why it works:** Every manager has heard a thousand "I'm passionate about technology" openers. None of them came with a live URL. Yours does. That's the differentiator the block trains without knowing it: receipts beat adjectives.

**Follow-up to expect:** "Walk me through that project." Have the project's README open in a tab. Walk the blocks: what it does, one hard thing you hit, how you fixed it. Two minutes max.

### 2. "What's your biggest weakness?"

**What they mean:** *Do you know yourself? Are you coachable? Will you gaslight me with "I work too hard"?*

**The pattern:** Real weakness → what you're doing about it → early proof it's working. The fake-weakness dodge ("perfectionist," "too dedicated") is a smell test — they've heard it a thousand times and it says "I don't answer hard questions honestly."

**The answer:**
> "Documentation. Early on I shipped fast and documented sloppy — the code worked but the next person couldn't read my trail. I've been fixing that by treating the README as part of the project, not the leftover — my recent repos all have setup steps a stranger could follow. It's now the part I write first."

**Why it works:** It's a real weakness every developer has, the fix is verifiable on your GitHub (they can literally read your READMEs), and it shows self-management without self-destruction.

**Follow-up to expect:** "Give me an example." Have the before/after: a project with a thin README vs. a recent one with a full one. Both on your profile. The weakness answer that survives the follow-up is the one that's true.

### 3. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a coworker/teammate."

**What they mean:** *Are you going to be a problem? Can you push back without breaking the room?*

**The pattern:** Situation → what you said → what happened → what you'd do differently. The trap is answers that end in "and I was right" — nobody hires the guy who needed to be right more than he needed the room to work.

**The answer:**
> "On a group project, a teammate wanted to ship a feature I thought was scope creep — we were already behind. Instead of arguing in the group chat, I asked him to walk me through his reasoning. Turned out he'd promised it to a user I didn't know about. We cut it to a smaller version that covered that user without breaking the deadline. What I'd do differently is ask 'what am I not seeing' earlier — I almost fought the right guy for the wrong reason."

**Why it works:** Curiosity before conflict, the room survived, and the lesson is about YOU, not him. On the block, "asking before swinging" is a skill that took years to learn — it reads as senior-level emotional intelligence in an office.

**Follow-up to expect:** "And if he'd been wrong anyway?" → "Then I'd take it to the person owning the deadline with both options on the table. Disagree, commit, and don't relitigate — the room moves."

### 4. "Why do you want to work here?"

**What they mean:** *Did you research us, or are we your 47th application?*

**The pattern:** One specific thing about THEM → one bridge to YOU → the future tense. Generic praise ("great culture!") is an auto-fail; specific praise shows you did homework they can check.

**The answer:**
> "You ship [specific product/feature — the one you actually use or studied]. I went through the onboarding flow last week and found [a real observation — 'the error states are the best I've seen, most teams treat them as afterthoughts']. I build the same way — my last project's error messages are written for the person having the worst day, not the developer. I want to do that work at the scale you're doing it."

**Why it works:** You cited a detail that can only come from actually looking. You connected it to receipts on your GitHub. And you closed in future tense — "I want to do that work here," not "I hope you pick me."

**Follow-up to expect:** "What would you improve?" Have ONE small, respectful observation. Never "your whole site is mid." One thing, framed as a question you'd ask on the team.

### 5. "Do you have any questions for me?"

**What they mean:** *Are you interviewing them too, or begging?* This is a filter question — "no, I'm good" reads as "I'll take anything."

**The questions that land (pick two):**
- "What does the first 90 days look like for the person who gets this seat? What's the one thing you'd want shipped by day 30?"
- "When you think about the people who've done best in this role — what did they do in their first year that the others didn't?"
- "What's the biggest technical debt on the team right now, and is fixing it on this seat's plate?" (Asks about the real work, signals you know codebases have history.)
- "How does the team handle disagreement about approach — code review culture, or lead decides?" (You already know the answer pattern from Question 3 — you're checking if they practice it.)

**The question that closes:**
> "Based on everything today — is there anything about my background that gives you pause? I'd rather answer it now than leave it in the room."

That's the closer because it's brave, it's coachable, and it hands you the last word in the room before you leave. Managers remember the candidate who asked it.

## The questions you HOPE they ask

**"What are you proud of?"** → Your pinned repo. The one you'd show your mother. Tell the story of building it — the constraint (phone-only? tethered 3G? 4GB refurb?) IS the story. Constraints as receipts: "I built this on a tethered phone connection because that's the rig I had — that's why it loads in under three seconds."

**"How do you keep learning?"** → The block itself. "I'm in a community where the rule is you ship weekly and you review each other's work — I've had 11 PRs merged this quarter and I've reviewed more than that." (Only say numbers that are true — they're checkable on your profile. But if they're true, they're GOLD.)

**"Tell me about a time you failed."** → Same pattern as disagreement: situation, what you did, what changed. The failure story that lands ends in a system change, not an apology: "Now I write the failing test first."

## The questions you DREAD — the real-talk section

### "Why the gap in your resume?"

The honest, strong answer, one breath, then pivot forward:
> "That period, I was dealing with a legal situation / incarceration / a family crisis / health. I used the time to get right and start building — everything on my GitHub is from the last [N] months. What I built since is the best picture of what I bring."

Then STOP. Do not over-explain. Interviewers ask follow-ups about the past when you dwell in it; they ask follow-ups about the future when you show them one. The resume coach's full playbook for this moment: prompts/resume-hustle/street_to_executive.txt and the Ban-the-Box map in fedpromptly-coach/prompts/resume_optimizer.txt.

### "We do background checks. Anything you want to tell us?"

If it's coming, it's coming — control the framing by going first, calm, rehearsed, with a receipt attached:
> "Yes. [One sentence on the record — date, nature, no adjectives.] It's from another chapter. What I've built since is [the GitHub, the projects, the refs]. I'd rather you hear it from me than a database."

Rehearsed beats improvised. The prepared sentence is the difference between a pause and a spiral. And target fair-chance employers from the start — the block's wiki keeps the running list (wiki/Hustling/).

### "What's your salary expectation?"

The floor question, in corporate form. The block's rule (PRICING.md): know your floor before the room. Research the role's band (levels.fyi, Glassdoor, the state's pay-transparency listings) BEFORE the interview, then:
> "Based on the market for this role in [city], I'm looking at [band]. Where does this seat sit in that range?"

Asking their band first is fair game and pay-transparency laws increasingly force an answer. Never a bare number with no research behind it. Never "whatever you think is fair" — that's handing the ledger to the other side of the table. The rate-floor instinct ($150 one-pager minimum) scales all the way up: know the number before the room.

## The day-of protocol

**The night before:** Read this sheet once. Pick your five answers. Say each one OUT LOUD once — out loud is a different memory than silent, and the room is out loud. Open your GitHub, pin your best repo (profile → Customize → pins), make sure the README of that repo is written for a stranger (it is — you're from the block, you document receipts).

**The morning of:** Shower, iron, one accessory that's YOU (the chain, the fresh cut, the clean kicks — whatever says "I'm not pretending to be somebody else"). The goal is not a costume. The goal is you, on a good day, translated.

**The hour before:** Read the five questions' headers only. No cramming — confidence compounds, second-guessing spirals. Eat something. Phone charged. GitHub tab pre-loaded.

**In the room:** They ask. You breathe. One beat. Then answer in the pattern. The beat before the answer is not weakness — it's the same beat a rapper takes before the bar lands. Timing is delivery, and delivery is half the answer.

**After:** Same-day thank-you email, three sentences: what you liked, what you'd bring, you're ready for next steps. Short is professional. Long is nervous.

## The one rule that runs under all of it

Every answer you give should hand them the same picture: a builder with receipts, coachable under pressure, who makes rooms work. Not a perfect candidate — a real one. The block didn't raise actors; it raised men who show up with proof. That's what they're actually hiring. The whole cheat sheet is just making sure they can see it.
